SEO reports train us to celebrate lines that go up and explain lines that go down.

That instinct is usually useful. It can also be completely wrong.

I recently worked through a case where a site had accumulated thousands of junk URLs after a security problem. Search engines discovered them, impressions jumped, and the top-level chart looked busy. It would have been easy to screenshot the growth and call it progress.

It was not progress. The site was becoming visible for pages it never wanted to have.

## An impression is not automatically valuable

Search Console counts visibility. It does not label every impression as relevant, profitable, or even desirable.

If spam pages begin appearing for unrelated searches, total impressions may rise while the site becomes less healthy. Average position can worsen, click-through rate can collapse, crawl activity can be wasted, and the useful pages can become harder to evaluate inside all that noise.

So the first job is not “recover the big number.” It is to recover the signal.

## Cleanup can make the graph look worse

The recovery work is not mysterious, but it needs care:

1. Fix the source of the unwanted URLs.
2. Make sure removed pages return the correct status.
3. Stop internal links and sitemaps from advertising junk.
4. Use search-engine removal tools where they help accelerate cleanup.
5. Re-submit and strengthen the pages that actually matter.
6. Watch query quality, clicks, CTR, and indexed-page patterns—not only impressions.

As irrelevant URLs disappear, impressions can drop sharply. In this situation, that decline is the chart becoming honest again.

A smaller pool of relevant impressions with a healthier click-through rate is a much better baseline than a huge pool created by spam.

## Read the story behind the metric

I like metrics, but a metric without its mechanism is just a number wearing a suit.

Before judging a change, I ask:

- Which queries created it?
- Which pages received it?
- Did real clicks move with the impressions?
- Did the site change, or did search engines change what they were showing?
- Is this growth we would intentionally try to reproduce?

That last question catches a lot of nonsense.

Good SEO is not forcing every line upward every week. Sometimes it is removing fake visibility, accepting an uglier short-term graph, and rebuilding from numbers that describe the real site.
